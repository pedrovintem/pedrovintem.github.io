Research Task 2: Brass Bands and Wind Bands

Brass bands: instrumentation and style

The British brass band has had a standard line-up since the end of the nineteenth century. It has 25 players plus percussion: nine B-flat cornets (four solo, two seconds, two thirds and one repiano), one E-flat soprano cornet, one flugel horn, three tenor horns, two baritones, two euphoniums, two E-flat basses, two B-flat basses, two tenor trombones and one bass trombone (Herbert, 1992). There are no woodwinds. Percussion was banned from contests for part of the twentieth century and came back in the 1960s (Herbert, 1992).

The style grew out of transcriptions. Herbert (1992) groups the repertoire into three types: classical music, especially Italian opera overtures and selections, popular dances such as quadrilles, schottisches and polkas, and functional pieces such as anthems, carols and folk tunes. Modern players are known for almost constant vibrato and crisp articulation (Herbert, 1992).

Wind bands: instrumentation and style

The wind band has no fixed line-up. Rhodes (2007) says that no common standard has ever been fully achieved, because band music differs from one country to the next. The ensemble can include flutes and piccolo, oboes, bassoons, a full family of clarinets from E-flat to contrabass, saxophones, cornets and trumpets, horns, trombones, euphoniums, tubas and percussion (Rhodes, 2007).

The balance changes by country. Rhodes (2007) quotes Revelli: Germany recommends one-third woodwinds, France and Italy one-half, England about one-half, and America two-thirds for concert bands. French and Italian bands use the full saxhorn family. British bands favour cornets over trumpets. American bands often use saxophones in place of bassoons.

Size changes too. Sousa’s band in 1892 had 14 B-flat clarinets, three saxophones and 29 players in the other sections. The University of Illinois band in 1938 had 24 B-flat clarinets and instruments such as the heckelphone and the sarrusophone (Rhodes, 2007). The wind ensemble, started by Frederick Fennell at Eastman in 1952, uses mostly one player per part to avoid heavy doubling (Rhodes, 2007).

The early wind band repertoire came mostly from orchestral literature (Rhodes, 2007). In the twentieth century composers wrote original works for the band. Rhodes mentions Holst’s Suite in E-flat, Grainger’s Lincolnshire Posy and Schuman’s George Washington Bridge.

Brass bands: history and development

Before the 1830s, bands of wind and string instruments played in rural Anglican churches. They declined in the 1830s, and bands that called themselves brass bands appeared (Herbert, 1992). Claims about the “first” brass band have no reliable evidence. In 1838 Robert Thompson Crawshay formed a band of 20 players at Cyfarthfa Castle, probably the first virtuoso British brass band (Herbert, 1992).

Herbert (1992) dates the movement to 1844. That year the Distin family gained the franchise to import Adolphe Sax’s valve instruments. Amateur playing increased and the first well-documented contest followed in 1845 at Burton Constable. Valve instruments were quick to learn and cheap to make. Cornopeans cost £5 to £10 around 1840 and new cornets cost about £3 in the 1850s. Besson made 52,000 instruments between 1862 and 1895 (Herbert, 1992).

Money and class both mattered. Middle-class reformers promoted music as a “rational recreation” and supplied instruments, repertoire and encouragement. Some industrialists sponsored bands. John Foster founded the Black Dyke Mills Band in 1855 for his mill workers (Herbert, 1992). Herbert argues that the idea of industrialists paying for everything is almost certainly untrue. Most bands raised money from subscriptions, concerts and contests, and hire purchase schemes helped them buy instruments. The working class gradually took control of the movement.

Contests shaped the sound. Railways and cheap excursion fares brought bandsmen from wide areas. The first Belle Vue contest in Manchester reportedly drew 24,000 people (Herbert, 1992). Contests led to standard playing styles and to a standard instrumentation. From the 1880s, three conductors, Edwin Swift, James Gladney and Alexander Owen, won most of the major prizes for about twenty years, and their line-up became the standard. Leading bands were semi-professional. The Besses o’ th’ Barn Band registered as a limited company in 1887 and toured the world (Herbert, 1992).

By 1900 brass bands had a clear identity, but they were set apart from mainstream British music. Herbert calls this a “cultural ghetto”. Their lasting influence is on players, since many of the best British orchestral brass players of the twentieth century began in brass bands (Herbert, 1992).

References

Herbert, T. (1992) ‘Victorian brass bands: the establishment of a “working class musical tradition”’, Historic Brass Society Journal pp. 1-11.

Rhodes, S.L. (2007) ‘Instrumentation’, in A history of the wind band. Available at: https://windbandhistory.neocities.org/index-2 (Accessed: 11 October 2026).
